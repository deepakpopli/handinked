# How does memory keep things?

Three jobs: get it in, keep it, get it back out.

```diagram

  {"id": "ltm", "label": "long-term\nmemory", "shape": "cylinder"}

 "edges": [["page", "wm"], ["wm", "ltm"], ["ltm", "wm", "recall", "dashed"]]}
```

- Working memory is small: about [[4 things]] at once. That is why each page here teaches a few ideas, not twenty.
- Reading practises the way in. The interview only tests the dashed arrow: the way __out__.

> key: train the way out. ==Recall is the skill, not reading.==

> say: I remember what I have pulled out of my head before, not what I have only looked at.

# Why do I forget what I read?

Forgetting is fastest right after learning. Then it slows down.

```diagram

 "events": [

  {"at": "hours", "label": "fastest loss"},
  {"at": "days", "label": "slower loss"},
  {"at": "weeks", "label": "what is left stays"}
 ],
 "notes": [{"to": "e1", "text": "so come back\nearly, then later"}]}
```

- rereading and highlighting: low value for the time they take
- testing yourself: better memory later, for the same time

> careful: a reread makes the page look familiar. Familiar is not known.

> say: Rereading makes the page feel known. Only recalling it shows me whether I know it.

# What works best?

Pull the answer out of your head, then check it.

1. Read the title question. ==Guess first==, even if you are unsure.
2. Read the page once.
3. Close it. Say the answer out loud in your own words.
4. Open it and check. Fix what you missed.

A wrong guess is fine. It helps, as long as you check the right answer straight after.

Why out loud? You remember best in the form you practised, and the interview is spoken.

> key: closed page, out loud, then check

> say: I guess, read once, close the page, answer out loud, and then check what I missed.

# When should I come back?

Several short sessions on different days beat one long one.

```diagram
{"kind": "timeline",
 "events": [
  {"at": "day 0", "label": "learn"},
  {"at": "day 2", "label": "recall 1"},
  {"at": "day 4", "label": "recall 2"},
  {"at": "day 6", "label": "run-through"},
  {"at": "day 7", "label": "interview"}
 ],
 "notes": [{"to": "e3", "text": "every question,\nout loud"}]}
```

- interview in about a week: come back every 1 to 2 days
- in 2 to 3 weeks: every 3 to 6 days
- aim to get each topic right in 3 separate sessions

> careful: do not trade sleep for study hours. A lost night hurts learning.

> say: I space my sessions over days, get each topic right three times, and sleep before the day.

# What should I not believe?

- ~~"You forget 70% in a day."~~ The curve is real. The fixed number is not.
- ~~"I am a visual learner."~~ Matching a learning style has not been shown to help. A good diagram helps everyone.
- ~~"One more all-nighter."~~ Lost sleep costs more than the hours give.
- ~~"Highlight more."~~ It marks the page, not your memory.

How sure is this? The main findings are solid, the gains moderate. Proof that mock interviews raise results is thin. Practising in the form of the test is well supported.

> note: Sources: Roediger and Karpicke 2006, Cepeda 2008, Dunlosky 2013, Cowan 2001, Kornell 2009, Rawson and Dunlosky 2011, Pashler 2008.

> key: questions first, page closed, out loud, spaced over days
